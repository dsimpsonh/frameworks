The STAKE Method

For a business that owns a vertical and needs a product it can't build. I build it and run it on low input; you sell it into the market you already stand in and keep the larger share. The method prices both halves so neither side is quietly subsidising the other.

Where this sits

The third method, for a different situation

LEVER and RECALL are diagnostics: a client pays to find out what to build. STAKE is a partnership: a business that already knows what it needs, owns the route to the buyer, and lacks the capability to build and run it. The unit of analysis is a revenue stream, and the question is whether it pays both parties for years — not whether it launches.

The partner

Owns the vertical

Standing in the market, credibility with the buyer, the selling relationship. They do the selling and take the larger share.

Me

Builds and runs it

System, domain method, hosting, updates, second-line support. Paid by a royalty for the asset and a service fee for the running — two lines, because they fall at different rates.

The origin

A Houston language-education company

Ten full-time staff and around fifty contract teachers. While automating their operations, the tools the founder needed turned out to be tools the whole industry needed. The case study →

The model

Two lines, two equations

A royalty pays for the asset and falls as the partner's cash covers more of the build. A service fee pays for the running and does not fall when the build is paid for — because the support calls arrive in year two regardless.

Ry = 20% × AS × (1 − 0.4c)

Royalty. AS = asset share from the Motion Ledger (0–1). c = share of the build funded in cash (0–1). Clamped to 5–18%.

Sv = 10% × RS

Service fee. RS = run share from the Motion Ledger (0–1). Clamped to 3–12%. Paid as a percentage with a monthly floor.

The Motion Ledger

Who does the work that produces each pound

Asset — system 50 · domain method 30 · concept 20 → AS.
Run — hosting 25 · updates 40 · second-line support 20 · first-line support 15 → RS.

Score each row by who actually does it. The shares fall out of the ledger; nobody negotiates them.

The floor

Floor = monthly run cost × 1.3

A pure revenue percentage is a second royalty wearing a different name, and it pays nothing in the year the support calls arrive. In the case: £350/month, with the percentage overtaking the floor at £52,174 of annual partner revenue.

The decision

Carry the stream if RQ ≥ 4

RQ = annual income ÷ annual run cost. Below four, the stream is a hobby with an invoice attached. Try it with your own numbers — the calculator runs the equations above.

Three findings

What the model taught me

01

Profitability is an engineering problem, not a negotiating one

At £100k of partner revenue: two hours a month to run it gives RQ 5.7 — carry. Eight hours gives RQ 2.2 — don't. The rate is irrelevant next to the hours.

02

“Passive revenue” is a three-to-six-year build

Raising maturity revenue from £100k to £150k pulls year six to year four — as powerful as doubling the signing rate. The take rate barely registers.

03

Reporting outranks the rate

Not honesty — capability. A business that invoices everything as one line cannot report product revenue separately even when it wants to. Build the reporting before the product.

Gates

Six reasons to walk away

  • TI < 0.50 on the Motion Ledger — the split isn't defensible.
  • No second buyer named — one customer is a project, not a product.
  • No service floor — the running is unpaid in the year it costs the most.
  • No reporting obligation — the royalty can't be verified, so it can't be collected.
  • RQ < 4 — the stream doesn't pay for its own upkeep.
  • First-line support drifting to you — the named failure mode of the whole model.
Structure

Royalties from a UK company, no US membership interest

Both lines are characterised as royalties paid to the UK company. No LLC membership interest in the partner. Structured for 0% withholding under Article 12 of the UK–US treaty. Not tax advice — confirm with counsel on both sides before signing.

The routing question

“When this goes wrong, is it because someone chose badly, or because someone couldn't find out?”

Chose badly → LEVER. Couldn't find out → RECALL. Neither, but you own a vertical and need a product → STAKE.
