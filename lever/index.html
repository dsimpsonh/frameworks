The LEVER Method

Start at the business goal. Blueprint the operation until the friction is visible. Score every friction moment for how much risk-adjusted money is actually recoverable there — then build the one bespoke ML tool that recovers it, in the one sequence that doesn't break something downstream.

Align the goal. Expose the friction. Sequence the fix.

The mechanism, restated

Data has no value. Decisions have value. Friction is where decisions leak value. Blueprinting makes the leak visible; the Leverage Equation prices it; the Ripple Matrix stops you plugging one leak and bursting the pipe somewhere else.

The recommendation

Lead with capacity-vs-demand operations

Not "logistics" as an industry label — position by operating model: businesses that allocate finite capacity (vehicles, engineers, machines, slots, stock, shifts) against variable demand. That cuts across 3PL, wholesale distribution, field service, facilities management and parts of manufacturing.

Five reasons it wins:

  • The labels already exist. Every order, job and delivery has a recorded outcome — late/on-time, first-time-fix, short-picked, returned. Data sufficiency is LEVER's binding constraint and this sector relaxes it by default.
  • Blueprinting was built for this. Physical flows with visible handoffs produce a friction map that a Managing Director recognises in four seconds.
  • Thin margins do your arithmetic. On 2–5% net margin, recovering £370k of leakage is a profit story, not an efficiency story.
  • Constraint theory maps natively. These are throughput systems, so your critical-path and ripple analysis is rigorous rather than metaphorical.
  • No competition at this tier. The Big Four ignore £20–250m ops businesses; the software vendors sell tools, not diagnosis. Nobody is selling the middle.
Beachhead, first two clients

Professional & B2B services

Scores one point lower but is materially easier to sell: founder-led decisions, no procurement, four-week cycles, and it's the third-highest AI-adopting UK sector (20–28%) so budget already exists. Weaker labels and a lower ceiling — use it to buy your first two case studies and the decision-lift report you need to charge £35k, then pivot the marketing to operations.

Deliberately excluded

Financial services and property

Both score respectably on the fundamentals and both are strategically wrong for you right now. Property is Aletheai's live pilot vertical; wealth is its next. Consulting there muddies the engine-neutrality that keeps LEVER publishable and keeps the September filing clean. Revisit after the PCT is in.

Stage L

The Goal Ladder

The alignment spine. Four rungs, built in week one and signed by the client before any mapping starts. Its job is to make orphaned work impossible: if a friction moment cannot be traced up to rung one, it does not enter the diagnostic — however painful it is.

Rung 1

The Goal

One business goal for the next 12–24 months, in the board's own words, with its number attached. Not three goals. One.

"Lift EBITDA from 6% to 9% by FY28 without adding headcount."
Rung 2

Value drivers

The three to five levers that arithmetically move that number. Derived, not brainstormed — you should be able to show the sum.

Throughput per vehicle · cost-to-serve per order · retention of top-quartile accounts · working-capital cycle
Rung 3

Outcome metrics

One measurable per driver, with today's baseline and the target. This is what the Run stage measures lift against, so it has to be something already instrumented — or instrumenting it becomes action #1.

Drops per route per day: 18.4 → 21.0 · Cost per order: £8.90 → £7.60
Rung 4

Moments

The specific operational moments on the Decision Blueprint that move each metric. Every moment carries the ID of the metric it serves. This is the join between strategy and operations, and it is the join everybody else skips.

M-07 "4pm allocation of next-day slots" → serves Drops/route/day and Cost per order
The one-line rule that makes alignment real

No moment enters the Ledger without a rung-4 link to rung 1. Say this out loud in the kickoff. It kills the client's pet projects diplomatically, kills your own scope creep, and means the final readout is structurally incapable of drifting from what the board asked for. It is also why the diagnostic can be delivered in four weeks.

The Friction Index — how a moment gets scored

Five dimensions, 0–5 each, observed not asked. Total 0–25. Friction is the discovery instrument: it decides which moments are worth costing. It is deliberately not a term in the money equation.

DelayWaiting time before or inside the moment
ReworkProportion that comes back round again
HandoffsNumber of swim-lane crossings it triggers
EffortManual touches, re-keying, spreadsheet gymnastics
StrainEmotional cost to customer or staff — predicts churn and attrition before the numbers do

FI ≥ 15 = cost it. FI ≥ 15 in the Decision lane with a broken Data lane = you have just found the client's most valuable blind spot.

Why the two new lanes are the whole innovation

Friction is visible. Judgement isn't.

A standard blueprint would show M-07 as a backstage task called "slot planning". The Decision lane reveals that a single planner is making 400 allocation judgements a day from tacit rules in their head. The Data lane reveals the spreadsheet is overwritten every night — so there is no record of what was decided or whether it worked.

That is simultaneously the highest-leverage moment in the business, the biggest single-person risk, and currently unbuildable. No data audit finds this. No AI-readiness score finds this. A blueprint with a Decision lane finds it in ninety minutes — and the recommendation writes itself: instrument first, model next quarter.

Stage V · The instrument

The Leverage Equation

One formula, six terms, output in pounds per year. Every term is separately arguable with the client, which is the point — you are not asking them to trust a black box, you are getting them to sign each number. The output is risk-adjusted recoverable annual value, not "AI opportunity".

L£  =  N × Δ × a  ×  (D × T × A)  ×  κ  ×  (1 − Λ)

Value at Stake × Solvability × Constraint position × Ripple discount

N

Occurrences

How many times a year this moment happens. From the system, never from an interview.

Δ

Gap cost

£ cost per occurrence of the gap between current and achievable — delay + rework + loss.

a

Decision-addressable share

0–1. The fraction of Δ that better information or judgement could remove. The rest is capacity or process, and pretending otherwise is how ML projects die.

D·T·A

Solvability

Data sufficiency × model tractability × adoption feasibility, each 0–1. Multiplied, not averaged — these are AND conditions, so any zero correctly kills the whole thing.

κ

Constraint position

1.0 if this moment is the system constraint or feeds it directly; 0.5 on the critical path; 0.15 off-constraint. Improving a non-bottleneck produces local tidiness, not throughput.

Λ

Ripple loss

0–1. The share of the gain expected to be re-absorbed elsewhere in the system. Read straight off the Ripple Matrix.

LQ

Leverage Quotient

L£ ÷ (build cost + year-one run cost). The ranking number. Build if LQ ≥ 3.

✓

The calibration rule

Every Δ and every a is signed off by a named client owner. That turns the model from your spreadsheet into their number — and makes the build fee unarguable.

Decision rule

LQ ≥ 3 → Build

Three times year-one cost. Below that the risk premium on a bespoke build isn't earned.

Decision rule

D < 0.4 → Instrument

High L£ but no labelled history means the answer is a three-week instrumentation project, then revisit next quarter. This is the honest recommendation that wins the follow-on.

Decision rule

a < 0.2 → Redesign

If less than a fifth of the cost is decision-addressable, it's a capacity or process problem. Service redesign, not a model. Say so.

Stage V · Systemic safety

The Ripple Matrix

In a complex operation, every fix redistributes load. Improve slot allocation and you push volume into a pick face that was already the second-tightest point in the system. The Ripple Matrix makes that arithmetic visible before anyone writes code — and it is the single hardest thing for a competitor to copy, because it requires the blueprint to exist first.

The three flags that change a recommendation

  • Constraint migration — the intervention loads a moment already at FI ≥ 15. The recommendation becomes a paired intervention, never a single one.
  • Sequencing conflict — two interventions load the same downstream moment. They cannot ship in the same quarter, whatever the ranking says.
  • Policy collision — the intervention's optimum contradicts a standing rule (a cut-off time, an SLA, a commission scheme). Someone has to change the policy first, and that someone is not you.

The output is not a ranked list. It is the shortest ordered sequence that moves the system constraint without triggering a migration flag — with the honest label on each step.

Sequenced later

The Next Best Action report

The diagnostic's closing artefact. Every recommendation is typed — and being honest that most moments are not ML problems is precisely what makes the one that is credible.

01

Instrument

The signal isn't recorded yet. Start capturing outcomes. Weeks, not months.

02

Redesign

Remove the moment entirely. Service design. No technology at all.

03

Automate

Deterministic rules. Cheap, transparent, and often enough.

04

Model

A bespoke ML decision tool. Where your Build fee lives — honestly, one or two moments in twenty-five.

05

Leave

Explicitly deprioritised, with the reason written down. Naming these is half the value.

Each of the top five carries: the action, its type, L£, LQ, prerequisites, ripple flags, a named owner, and the single measurement that will prove it worked. Plus a "do not do these, and why" section — the part clients quote back to you eighteen months later.

Stages E & R

Engineer and Run

Unchanged from v1 in structure, now fed by a sequenced plan instead of a ranked list — which is what makes fixed-fee builds safe to quote.

Phase 2 · The LEVER Build · 6–10 weeks per tool
EStage 04

Engineer Archetype → algorithm → interface

The decision archetype prescribes the algorithm family and the interface pattern. Eight archetypes: Prioritise, Forecast, Classify, Detect, Segment, Allocate, Extract, Match — each with a laddered algorithm path (heuristic → linear → gradient boosting → specialist) and a matching UI pattern (ranked worklist, forecast chart, flagged queue, exception feed, cohort explorer, what-if calculator, review queue, suggested pairs).

  • Build the decision table: one row per historical decision instance, its context at the time, and the outcome — with no leakage of future information
  • Ship a transparent heuristic baseline in week one and measure it
  • Backtest against real past decisions; the model ships only if it beats the baseline
  • Five-layer reference stack: Ingest → Ground truth → Model → Serve → Interface, single-tenant, your reusable app shell re-skinned
The rule that protects your margin: the baseline always ships first. Roughly a third of the time the honest answer is "you didn't need ML for this one" — the most trust-building sentence available to you, and the reason the diagnostic must be priced to stand alone.
RStage 05

Run Embed, measure, compound

Adoption is a design problem, not a training problem — which is exactly where a design researcher beats a data scientist.

  • Embed into the existing ritual: the morning list, the 4pm planning slot, the depot standup
  • Capture the human's actual decision in the interface — it becomes next quarter's labelled training data (the Decision Loop)
  • Measure lift against the rung-3 baseline at 30 / 60 / 90 days, and re-run the Ripple Matrix to confirm the constraint moved where you predicted
  • Hand over, or transition to the Run retainer
Output: decision-lift report tied to the signed Goal Ladder — the artefact that makes tool #2 a conversation rather than a pitch, and lets you raise your Diagnostic price.

The pricing rules

Never quote days.

Quote moments, deliverables and dates. A day rate on the page turns you into a contractor.

Always three options.

Scan / Diagnostic / Diagnostic + Build. The question becomes "which one", never "whether".

Anchor the Build to L£, not to effort.

12–18% of a number they signed in the Diagnostic. You are pricing their recovery, not your hours.

Credits, never discounts.

Crediting the Scan protects the rate and creates a 30-day clock. Discounting destroys both.

Protection

IP and disclosure guards

Non-negotiable while the September clock runs

  • LEVER is engine-neutral: standard open-source tooling only. No Aletheai core code, no behavioural primitives, no claim language in any client-facing document.
  • Contracts: the client owns their data and the model trained on it. You own the method, the Goal Ladder, the Decision Blueprint format, the Friction Index, the Leverage Equation, the Ripple Matrix, the templates and the app shell — licensed to them for their instance.

  • Do not consult in property or wealth before the PCT is filed. Both are Aletheai verticals; the separation has to be visible, not merely intended.
  • Publishing LEVER publicly is safe and desirable because it contains none of the protected material. Keep it that way on purpose.
